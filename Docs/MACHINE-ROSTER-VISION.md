# Machine roster — the user's design vision

Provenance: user message, 2026-11-17, pasted verbatim: "These are the machines I'd be most interested in developing." This is the
authoritative statement of intent for the twelve proposed machines. [MACHINE-SYSTEMS-DESIGN.md](MACHINE-SYSTEMS-DESIGN.md) keeps
the dependency matrix and status; this file keeps the player-facing pitch each machine must eventually honour. Where the two
differ, the vision describes the goal and the design document describes what is verified and what is missing.

Recurring themes the user states or implies, to be treated as design requirements:

- **Choices about what survives**: salvage, fractions, reconstruction and samples are decisions with competing finite outcomes.
- **Inputs decide outputs**: recipes follow what actually went into the machine (the tank, the vat, the sample), so the same
  machine serves different places differently.
- **Ordinary convenience stays**: ordinary repair, healing, carrying and basic item descriptions remain freely available; the
  machine earns its place through larger or deeper jobs, never by degrading the ordinary path.
- **Bounded, authored, local**: reservoirs, spaces, signals and consequences are bounded and authored, not full simulations.
- **Local consequence**: redirected water, heat or power changes the place around the machine, for better and worse.
- **Expedition and base relevance**: portable and installed versions, second bases (a listening post), connecting scattered
  places (a restored tower), keeping ordinary homes viable with a modest fuel-powered version. This is the bridge to the
  home/base system.
- **Visible state**: the machine reflects what is inside it (the vat shows what lives there).

## The twelve

1. **Forge and reclamation rig — choose what survives the salvage.**
   Extend the existing forge into a place for recovering useful components from equipment and machinery. A broken device might
   yield an intact pressure regulator, several plates, or a small amount of rare alloy — but recovering one could sacrifice the
   others. Knowledge and better tools improve your options. Suddenly, a ruined workshop contains recognizable opportunities
   rather than interchangeable scrap.

2. **Fractionating still — separate valuable mixtures.**
   Process contaminated water, oils, sludge, sap, and other collected liquids into different fractions. Choose between
   maximizing usable water, preserving a rare reagent, or producing a concentrated industrial solvent. Recipes depend on what
   actually went into the tank. A swamp workshop and a desert workshop could use the same machine for very different purposes.

3. **Restoration cradle — rebuild unusual equipment.**
   Hold a damaged artifact or machine while you replace missing assemblies. Substitutions change its behavior: a salvaged
   cooling unit might make a machine quieter but slower; an oversized motor could improve throughput while demanding more
   power. Restoring a find becomes a small design problem. Ordinary equipment repairs should remain convenient; the cradle
   earns its place through these larger reconstruction jobs.

4. **Assay cabinet — spend a sample to answer a useful question.**
   Analyze a material, liquid, organism, or machine fragment. Questions could include "Which component can I recover?", "Will
   this fluid corrode my pump?", or "What substance would neutralize this secretion?" Basic item descriptions remain freely
   available. The machine provides deeper, actionable knowledge, with the player choosing which question is worth consuming a
   scarce sample.

5. **Culture vat — cultivate something strange.**
   Use a collected organism and finite feedstock to grow useful material: sealing resin, luminous tissue, filtering membranes,
   medicinal cultures, or conductive fibers. Conditions determine the result. You might encourage rapid growth for a large
   quantity of ordinary material, or carefully maintain a smaller culture for a rare property. Wild specimens become meaningful
   finds, and the machine visibly reflects what lives inside it.

6. **Anchored capstan — move things you could never carry.**
   Attach a cable to heavy machinery, wreckage, gates, or other supported objects. It needs a sound anchor and a workable
   pulling route. This turns recovering a machine into an expedition: clear the doorway, brace the capstan, maneuver the load,
   and get it onto transport. The same device can open a passage or recover something from a pit. Portable versions would
   handle smaller loads.

7. **Sump pump — redirect water and other liquids.**
   Drain a flooded room, fill a cistern, supply a still, or lower the water around submerged machinery. The liquid needs a
   destination, and unsuitable fluids can require special fittings. Pumping out an abandoned basement could expose a useful
   room while making the courtyard outside wetter. This could operate through bounded reservoirs and authored spaces without
   requiring a full fluid simulation.

8. **Thermal exchanger — put local heat or cold to work.**
   Capture heat from a vent or furnace, or draw cooling from an appropriate source. Use it for drying materials, supporting a
   culture, cooling machinery, or preparing a sheltered workspace. Redirecting heat creates a local consequence: the workroom
   becomes comfortable while the exhaust area becomes hazardous. A modest fuel-powered version keeps ordinary homes viable;
   unusual locations offer stronger opportunities.

9. **Dynamo and charging bench — prepare portable power.**
   Charge cells and equipment using hand power, fuel, or suitable environmental sources. Early operation should be
   straightforward: connect a source, insert a cell, select a job. Different generators offer clear tradeoffs in portability,
   noise, fuel, and output. This creates an expedition decision: spend your remaining charge on a scanner, a pump, or restoring
   the machine you just found.

10. **Survey resonator — investigate what lies nearby.**
    Send a pulse into the ground and interpret the return. It might suggest a large cavity below, a metallic structure to the
    northeast, or a buried mechanism with a recognizable signature. Results would be approximate and depend on terrain,
    calibration, and available samples. It gives you a lead worth investigating while leaving the actual discovery to
    exploration. An underground listening post becomes a useful second base.

11. **Signal relay — connect your scattered places.**
    Receive reports, contact known people, arrange deliveries, and exchange observations between established sites. Coverage
    could depend on elevation, interference, and repaired infrastructure. Goods still travel through real arrangements and
    routes. An old tower might become valuable because restoring its transmitter connects your remote workshop with a
    settlement where you have trading relationships.

12. **Treatment bath — prepare for particular environments.**
    Use reagents to remove a specific contamination or apply a temporary protective treatment before an expedition. Perhaps a
    mineral bath reduces vulnerability to a particular corrosive secretion, while another treatment helps equipment shed
    clinging spores. The interesting choice is preparation for a known destination. Normal healing and recovery should remain
    accessible without having to return to your bath after every outing.

## Reading these against the lore authority

The pitch uses technological words (pressure regulator, motor, cells, transmitter, power). The canon world is the god-tree /
tepui world of `Lore/`; its material culture, factions and protected mysteries govern naming, voice and what an instrument may
claim to know (see the lore section of [MACHINE-SYSTEMS-DESIGN.md](MACHINE-SYSTEMS-DESIGN.md)). Each machine's in-world name,
parts and operators must be re-grounded in canon before it ships; the pitch fixes the player experience, not the fiction's nouns.
